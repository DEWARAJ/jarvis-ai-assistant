OVERVIEW

Telugu is a Dravidian language spoken primarily in the Indian states of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and is one of the most widely spoken languages in India, with approximately 85–95 million native speakers globally. It is the official language of both AP and Telangana, and has classical language status granted by the Government of India in 2008. Telugu is notable for its entirely indigenous script, its rich literary tradition dating back over a thousand years, and its musical phonetic quality, earning it the informal title "Italian of the East" for its vowel-ending words. It has produced significant literary, cinematic, and scholarly output, and is a living, evolving language used in technology, media, education, and daily life.

CORE CONCEPTS

Script and Phonology

Telugu uses an abugida script, meaning consonants carry an inherent vowel sound (typically 'a'), and vowel markers modify this default. The script is derived from the Brahmi script family, closely related to Kannada script. It consists of:
- 16 independent vowels (swaras): a, aa, i, ii, u, uu, ru, e, ee, ai, o, oo, au, am, ah, and the anusvara
- 36 consonants (vyanjanas), each with a full form and a conjunct form
- Vowel diacritics (gunintaalu) that attach to consonants to change the inherent vowel
- Special characters like anusvara (dot sound, written as sunnaa) and visarga
- Conjunct consonants (vaththulu) formed by combining two or more consonants, often used in Sanskrit-origin words

Vowels ending words is structurally common in Telugu, making it sound fluid and melodic. Consonant clusters rarely appear at word endings in native Telugu words.

Grammatical Structure

Telugu follows Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) word order.
- Nouns are inflected for case (nominative, accusative, instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, locative, vocative — roughly eight cases)
- There is a formal system of grammatical gender: masculine, feminine, and neuter. Rational beings (humans, divine beings) are masculine or feminine; everything else is generally neuter
- Number: singular and plural
- Pronouns distinguish formality levels rigorously — naenu (I), meeru (you formal), nuvvu (you informal), ayana (he formal), atanu (he informal), aame (she formal), aadi (she informal/neutral), vaaru (they formal)
- Verbs agree with subject in person, number, and gender
- Tense: past, present, future, marked through suffixes on verb roots
- Aspect and mood are expressed through verb suffixes and auxiliary verbs
